How to use this plan

  1. Open "This week" before a session. Everything you need for today is on that one page, including the exact words to say.
  2. Tick the rhythm, not every repetition. One tap per session is enough. Counting every treat is not the goal.
  3. Log the moments that matter. A win, a yellow or red moment, a new place. Add a photo or video link when you have one. Anyone helping with Wally can add notes too.
  4. Reflect at the end of the week. Answer the reflection prompts, then bring them to Claude. We decide together to repeat, split, simplify or move on.
The golden rule: a week ending is never a reason to move on. Wally's evidence is.

How suggestions are rated

Each major suggestion carries a reference strength. These ratings are Claude's synthesis of the sources, not a score given by any authority.

RatingMeaning
5/5Direct benchmark or strong consensus. Explicit in a public-access standard, veterinary behaviour guidance, or strong professional agreement.
4/5Strong supporting practice. Builds directly toward a benchmark and is widely recommended by reward-based professionals, though the exact exercise is not prescribed.
3/5Useful foundation. Sensible skill-building that supports the goal, not a formal benchmark.
2/5Optional enhancement. Handy for Wally's life, not essential.
1/5Enrichment or trick. Mainly for fun and relationship.

Progress stages

Four honest stages instead of percentages. One great session never makes a skill Reliable.

IntroducedLearning the game.
EmergingDoes it somewhere easy, with frequent rewards.
DevelopingDoes it in more than one place, with mild distraction.
ReliableDoes it consistently wherever it's needed.

When to call in a professional

Talk to your vet or a qualified reward-based behaviour professional if you see any of these:

  • Panic or growing distress when alone (escape attempts, heavy drooling, accidents, refusing food)
  • Biting, snapping or guarding food or toys
  • A sudden change in behaviour, or signs of pain or illness
  • Fear or barking that is getting worse despite distance and good management
References used for ratings
  • Government of Alberta: Get a service dog assessed (voluntary reference only)
  • AVSAB position statements, including Humane Dog Training (2021) and Puppy Socialization
This week · Week 1 · from Mon 19 Oct

Notice me, notice the world

Wally is four months old and the world is very exciting. This week we teach him that checking in with you pays, that he can look at a dog or person and then look away, and that coming when called is the best game in the house. Small wins, lots of them.

Three main skills

Skill 1Stage: Emerging

Check-ins and the name game

Reference strength4/5 Alberta's assessment expects the handler to keep control and the dog to stay calm in busy places; attention to the handler is the base of that. The exact game is common reward-based practice.
BaselineOffers check-ins in quiet places. Loses focus quickly with distraction.
  1. Free check-ins (indoors): any time Wally looks up at you on his own, say "Yes!" and drop a treat at your feet.
  2. Name game: when he's mildly distracted (sniffing the floor), say "Wally" once. Head turn toward you: "Yes!" and treat. No turn: wait 3 seconds, then make a kissy sound to help. Don't repeat his name.
  3. 5 to 8 reps, then "Free". Stop while he still wants more.
Say: Wally → he turns → Yes! → treat at your feet → Free
Ready to move outside when: head turn within about 2 seconds on 8 of 10 name calls, indoors, in 3 sessions in a row. (Training criterion, not an official standard.)

Skill 2Stage: Introduced

Calm watching: look, then look back

Reference strength4/5 Alberta's assessment looks for calm behaviour around crowds and other animals and a dog that does not attract attention. This exercise builds toward that, starting far away.
BaselineBarks at passing people or dogs on walks. Cause not yet clear (excitement and frustration are likely at this age); we watch and log, we don't label.
  1. Choose a spot where people or dogs pass far enough away that Wally can still eat: across a street, a park edge, a car window.
  2. Wally looks at the person or dog → "Yes!" → treat near your leg. The "Yes!" pays for looking calmly.
  3. After a few reps, pause a second after he looks. If he looks back to you by himself, "Yes!" and a better treat.
  4. 3 to 5 sightings per walk is plenty. Then walk on or "Go sniff".
Wally looks at a dog → Yes! → treat. Starting to stiffen → Let's go and turn away happily.
Ready to move a little closer when: takes food within 3 seconds of a sighting, no barking, on 3 walks in a row at the same distance. (Training criterion.)
Walks that hit it:
Skill 3Stage: Emerging

Recall: puppy ping-pong

Reference strength4/5 A reliable recall is a core safety skill in reward-based programmes and supports handler control. The ping-pong format is a practitioner game, not a prescribed test.
BaselineEmerging recall in easy settings.
  1. Two people, about 3 metres apart, indoors. One person says "Wally, come!" once, in a happy voice, and backs up a step.
  2. When he arrives, gently touch his collar, "Yes!", then 2 or 3 treats one after another.
  3. Other person calls. 4 to 6 recalls total, then "Free" and play.
  4. Solo version: toss a treat away, and as he finishes it, call him back.
Wally, come! → he arrives → collar touch → Yes! → treat, treat, treat → Free
Ready for the yard when: comes on the first call 8 of 10 times indoors, in 3 sessions in a row. (Training criterion.)
Sessions that hit it:

Protect the word: this week, don't use "Wally, come!" for bath time, nail trims or ending fun. Go and get him instead.

Bonus, only if it's going well3/5 Useful foundation

Meet the mat

Put a towel or small mat on the floor. Reward Wally any time he steps on it, then sits or lies on it. Drop treats between his front paws. 1 minute, then "Free". No cue word yet; we are teaching him the mat is a great place to be. It leads to settling in cafés and at the vet much later.

Daily rhythm

Tap once per session. Skipping a slot is fine; aim for most days, not perfect days. Ticks are remembered on this phone only.

Slot

This week's focus outside training

Management · 4/5

No on-leash greetings

Every leash greeting right now is a rehearsal of pulling, jumping and barking. For this week:

  • Walk at quieter times or on quieter routes
  • Cross the street or step behind a parked car when a dog comes
  • Friendly line for people: "He's in training, so we're saying hi from here today!"

Management is not training. It stops the old habit growing while the new one is built.

Reward experiment · 3/5

The taste test and the sniff test

  • Taste test: put two different treats in your closed hands, let him choose. Repeat with 4 or 5 foods to find his low, medium and top favourites.
  • Sniff test: on a walk, after a check-in, say "Go sniff" and let him sniff for 5 seconds. Does he check in again faster next time? Then sniffing is a reward.

Log what you learn below with the "Win" tag. This starts the Reward bank. AVSAB recommends reward-based methods alone.

Baseline only · 4/5

Being alone: just observe

No alone-time training yet. Once this week, film a short, ordinary absence (take out the recycling) with a phone or camera. Note: did he vocalise, pace, settle, eat? How long until he relaxed after you returned?

Come back before distress builds. If you see panic, escape attempts or heavy drooling, talk to your vet.

Rest

Decompression daily

One slow, sniffy wander or a long chew or lick mat every day, with no training asked. Tired puppies bark and bite more; rest is part of the plan.

Pocket guide: barking on walks

Green: notices, still loose, still eats. "Yes!" for the calm look, treat by your leg, let him look again, carry on.
Yellow: stares, leans, mouth closes, leash tightens. Add distance now. "Let's go", turn away cheerfully, put a car or hedge between you. Reward when he reconnects.
Red: barking, lunging, won't eat. Calmly leave. No scolding, no forced sit. Let him recover. Log the trigger, distance and recovery time, and make the next walk easier.

Signs this week is too hard

  • He stops taking treats, or snatches them hard
  • More barking or zoomies during or after sessions
  • He wanders off or sniffs away from you during indoor games
  • Recovery after a trigger takes longer than a minute or two

What to do: make it easier. More distance, a quieter room, better treats, shorter sessions. That's the plan working, not failing.
